# Bank and FMI use of Besu, 2024-2026

Research output for investigation `investigation-onchain-data-protection` (2026-11-23).

In 2024-2026, banks and financial market infrastructures (FMIs) use Besu mainly in permissioned networks that one regulated operator runs or that a small set of identified institutions validates. The production cases with independent or primary-source evidence are **Citi Token Services** (tokenized interbranch deposits, commercially live in select jurisdictions by October 2024) and the **Swift shared ledger** (MVP live July 2026; first live interbank tokenized-deposit transaction between HSBC and Standard Chartered on 20 August 2026). Most other bank uses are trials or pilots: the 2024 Eurosystem exploratory trials (SWIAT bonds and repo, the Iberclear/BME bond platform, Adhara-built deposit networks at UBS and Deutsche Bank, the HKMA wholesale CBDC network, DZ BANK derivatives), BIS Project Agorá, Banque de France/Euroclear Project Pythagore and Bank of Korea Project Hangang. The largest central-bank Besu program, **Brazil's Drex, was shut down in November 2025** after the central bank could not find a privacy design that hid data from third parties, kept it visible to authorities and complied with Brazil's data-protection law at scale. Tessera private transactions were deprecated by the Besu maintainers in September 2024, so the privacy approaches in current use are separate networks per bank or jurisdiction, keeping balances off the shared ledger, and Paladin, whose only documented institutional use is the Agorá prototype (Pente and Noto, no Zeto). Repo and collateral on Besu exist only as trials (SWIAT) or as systems without published status (DTCC Collateral AppChain, Clearstream D7). For a CDM collateral prototype that plans Pente privacy groups per deal, the main findings are that Paladin hides deal content from non-participants but leaves timing and participation metadata visible to every validator, that a Noto notary sees all state of its token, that Pente group membership is fixed when the group is created, and that Paladin is pre-V1 with all maintainers from one vendor. Claims marked **[LFDT report only]** rest solely on the Ledger Insights report commissioned by LF Decentralized Trust (LFDT), the foundation that hosts Besu; that report lists many institutions by platform without status or volume, tags some entries with more than one platform, and has an interest in counting deployments ([LFDT/Ledger Insights report](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/LFDT-institutional-adoption.pdf)).

## Two production systems and about a dozen trials make up bank use of Besu

The best-documented production system is **Citi Token Services for Cash**, which provides 24/7 real-time USD payments and settlement using tokenized interbranch deposits. Clients do not hold tokens. It runs on an internal private Besu network, and Citi cites the access controls and privacy of that private network as a reason for the design. Citi chose Besu because it is an Ethereum client, is open source under LFDT governance ("allows us control of the source code"), and is used by other financial institutions; Citi also "tuned the consensus mechanism and block creation process" for throughput, without publishing a figure. Citi Token Services for Trade, which adds smart-contract conditional payments, is a pilot ([LFDT Citi case study](https://www.lfdecentralizedtrust.org/case-studies/citi-transforms-transaction-banking-services-with-besu)). The case study is itself LFDT-published material. The statements that the service runs on CIDAP, has been live since April 2024 and handles **close to $1 billion per day** are **[LFDT report only]** ([LFDT/Ledger Insights report](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/LFDT-institutional-adoption.pdf)).

The **Swift shared ledger** was announced on 29 September 2025, with Consensys building the prototype and more than 30 institutions designing it, including Bank of America, BNP Paribas, BNY, Citi, Deutsche Bank, HSBC, JPMorgan Chase, MUFG, Santander and Wells Fargo; the press release named no ledger client ([Swift](https://www.swift.com/about-us/media-centre/press-releases/swift-add-blockchain-based-ledger-its-infrastructure-stack-groundbreaking-move-accelerate-and-scale-benefits-digital-finance-across-more-200-countries-and-territories-worldwide)). Finadium reported in April 2026 that the MVP uses an "EVM-compatible architecture based on Hyperledger Besu" ([Finadium](https://finadium.com/swift-announces-mvp-go-live-of-open-source-blockchain-based-ledger-in-2026/)). On 20 August 2026, **HSBC and Standard Chartered executed the first live cross-border bank-to-bank tokenized-deposit transaction** on it: payment messages went over the Swift ledger, obligations were recorded on each bank's own tokenized-deposit system and then matched and netted, and final settlement went through existing systems. Seventeen banks are preparing to pilot live transactions ([HSBC](https://www.about.hsbc.com.hk/news-and-media/hsbc-and-standard-chartered-execute-first-live-tokenised-deposit-transaction-on-swifts-blockchain)). In this design the shared Besu ledger holds messaging, matching and netting records, and balances stay on bank systems. A 2025 report that Swift was testing the Consensys Layer 2 network Linea ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/372591/swift-experiments-with-onchain-migration-using-ethereum-layer-2-linea-report)) is not reconciled with the 2026 Besu MVP in any source found.

The 2024 Eurosystem exploratory trials, documented by the ECB in a primary source that names each participant's ledger, provide most of the other primary evidence ([ECB Annex II](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/other/ecb.exploratoryworknewtechnologies202506_annex02.en.pdf)). **SWIAT** (DekaBank, LBBW, BayernLB) runs a private permissioned chain built on Besu with IBFT 2.0 and ran bond DvP trials, including a Siemens bond. The **BME Digital Bond Platform** of Iberclear (SIX group), with ten Spanish banks, runs on Besu with ERC-1410 and processed more than 200 transactions over three months covering issuance, OTC settlement, coupons and redemption. **UBS and Deutsche Bank each ran a private Besu network supplied by Adhara** for cross-border tokenized-deposit payments in EUR, CHF and GBP, with orchestration contracts on a third network. **DZ BANK** settled smart derivative contracts with an ERC-6123 contract on Besu twice daily over several days. The **HKMA wholesale CBDC network** runs on Besu and was used for EUR/HKD PvP with Swift as the interoperability provider. Goldman Sachs DAP is described as built on Canton with Daml and "underpinned by Hyperledger Besu".

Several further Besu attributions appear only in the LFDT report. These are **[LFDT report only]**: the Regulated Layer One (RL1)/SWIAT figures of 10 participants, 50 transactions and more than €700 million issued, plus KfW's plan to move a €100 million bond from Polygon to RL1; the **Fnality Payment System** on Besu, live since 2023 with a UK settlement finality designation in late 2024 (Fnality's own pages fetched do not name Besu); **Clearstream D7** on Besu (the ECB annex says D7's chain "is not publicly detailed"); the DTCC Tokenization Service and DTCC Collateral AppChain on Besu; UBS Digital Cash and UBS Gold Network; and a long list of entries without status, including Mastercard Multi-Token Network, DCJPY, Northern Trust Matrix Zenith, Marketnode and several Korean firms ([LFDT/Ledger Insights report](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/LFDT-institutional-adoption.pdf)).

Several of the institutions named in the question run other ledgers. JPMorgan's deposit token JPMD became available to institutional clients on Coinbase's public Layer 2 network Base on 12 November 2025, and the sources do not state what client Kinexys' permissioned chain uses ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/business/2025-11-12-jpmorgan-deposit-token-jpm-coin-378493)). In the ECB trials, Kinexys used an unnamed EVM test network, SG-FORGE used public Ethereum, Euroclear D-FMI used R3 Corda, BNP Paribas Neobonds used Daml/Canton, and IZNES used Fabric ([ECB Annex II](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/other/ecb.exploratoryworknewtechnologies202506_annex02.en.pdf)). The Eurosystem's own DLT-to-T2 link, Project Pontes, runs on Fabric; SWIAT is one of the Besu-based operators that connects to it **[LFDT report only]**. No Besu trade-finance platform was found; the trade-finance closures of 2022-2023 (Contour and Marco Polo on Corda, and we.trade) did not run on Besu and ended mainly for funding and adoption reasons ([GTR on Contour](https://www.gtreview.com/news/fintech/exclusive-contour-to-shut-down-as-bank-shareholders-pull-funding/); [GTR on Marco Polo](https://www.gtreview.com/news/fintech/marco-polo-brings-in-liquidators-as-funds-run-dry/)).

## Central banks moved Besu work from Brazil to Agorá, Pythagore and Hangang

**Drex** ran phase 1 from July 2023 to October 2024 on **Besu 24.1.1 with QBFT and 26 nodes** (10 run by the central bank, of which 6 were validators, and 16 run by participants), reachable only over Brazil's private financial network ([BCB Drex phase 1 report](https://www.bcb.gov.br/content/estabilidadefinanceira/real_digital_docs/piloto/Relatorio_Drex_piloto_fase_1.pdf)). The central bank tested Anonymous Zether, Rayls, Starlight and Microsoft Nova. It found that the tokens' authority could not track, locate, block or move funds under Zether and Rayls, that Rayls flagged irregularities only after execution and took 1 to 4 minutes for atomic DvP, and that a lost Zether key makes the token unmovable (same source). A central bank official reported about **150 TPS without privacy and under 10 TPS with a privacy solution**, against about 300 TPS for the existing RTGS, and named the result a "privacy trilemma" ([BIS-hosted speech, May 2025](https://www.bis.org/review/r250703g.htm)). Phase 2 ended in July 2025, and the next phase was narrowed to lien and collateral reconciliation tied to Pix without blockchain ([Ledger Insights, Aug 2025](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/brazils-drex-cbdc-project-pivots-away-from-blockchain-tokenization/)). On 4 November 2025 participants were told the blockchain infrastructure would be deactivated; an anonymous participant called the model insecure by central bank standards and costly ([Forbes Brasil](https://forbes.com.br/escolhas-do-editor/2025/11/banco-central-abandona-o-blockchain-do-drex-o-projeto-do-real-digital/)). Reporting also cited the inability to guarantee confidentiality under the LGPD data-protection law, the conflict between ledger immutability and erasure rights, and the unmet requirement that identities be visible to the central bank and authorized institutions and hidden from third parties; a market participant said the problem was not specific to Besu ([InvestNews](https://investnews.com.br/economia/por-que-o-bc-desligou-o-drex/)). The central bank has not published an official rationale, so the stated reasons come from press and participants.

**BIS Project Agorá** places tokenized commercial bank deposits on a shared unifying ledger and tokenized reserves on separate ledgers under each central bank, with seven central banks and more than 40 financial institutions in the prototype and no timeline for production ([Ledger Insights, May 2026](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/bis-project-agora-prototype-keeps-central-bank-money-under-domestic-control/)). The BIS prototype report states that both ledger types use QBFT in a proof-of-authority setup, that validators are identified institutions chosen off-chain, and that governance and liability for the unifying ledger were outside the prototype's scope ([BIS Agorá report](https://www.bis.org/publications/project-agora-shared-programmable-platform-wholesale-cross-border-payments.pdf)). The July 2026 real-value test, with five central banks, about 22 institutions and Kaleido as technology provider, ran **30 transactions totalling about CHF 800,000 across six currencies with an average of about 80 seconds** from initiation to settlement ([BIS RVT page](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/fmis/agora/rvt.htm)). A SUERF policy note by Jan Camenisch and Thomas Moser (SNB) confirms the use of Paladin with Pente and Noto and without Zeto ([SUERF Note 420](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/privacy-in-wholesale-cross-border-payments-assessing-project-agora/)). The statement that Agorá runs on the Besu client specifically is **[LFDT report only]**, because the BIS summary pages, the SUERF note and Kaleido's customer story do not name Besu.

**Pythagore** (Banque de France and Euroclear, 31 participants, commercial paper settled in wholesale CBDC through Pontes, pilot planned for late 2026) and **Hangang** (Bank of Korea as sole validator, ERC-20 wholesale CBDC and deposit tokens, phase 2 from March 2026 with up to 500,000 wallets) are both **[LFDT report only]** as to their use of Besu ([LFDT/Ledger Insights report](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/LFDT-institutional-adoption.pdf)). Neither source describes their privacy model. mBridge used a custom EVM-compatible ledger, and RBA Project Acacia used other platforms; neither used Besu according to the sources found ([BIS mBridge](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/cbdc/mcbdc_bridge.htm); [RBA Acacia report](https://rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/central-bank-digital-currency/pdf/project-acacia-final-report.pdf)).

## Repo and collateral on Besu remain at trial stage

The only primary-source repo and collateral trials on Besu are **SWIAT's** 2024 trials in the Eurosystem program: an overnight bilateral repo with Deka as collateral receiver and LBBW as cash receiver through the "Collateral Hub by SWIAT", and an intraday bilateral repo with NatWest as collateral receiver and DekaBank as cash receiver, tested with both an on-ramped traditional security and a native digital security. The ECB published learning objectives and no results ([ECB Annex II §3.21](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/other/ecb.exploratoryworknewtechnologies202506_annex02.en.pdf)). RL1's planned collateral functions, the **DTCC Collateral AppChain** (which records pledges as encumbrances on tokens while the assets stay on their home ledgers), and Clearstream D7's 23-hour collateral management are all **[LFDT report only]** as Besu systems, and none has published status or volume there ([LFDT/Ledger Insights report](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/LFDT-institutional-adoption.pdf)). DTCC announced its collateral platform in April 2025 ([DTCC](https://www.dtcc.com/news/2025/april/02/dtcc-announces-new-platform-for-tokenized-real-time-collateral-management)); that announcement was not checked for a Besu reference.

The most prominent recent tokenized-collateral event used an unnamed ledger. In October 2026, BNY recorded a tokenized 2-year Treasury note bought by Citadel Securities on BNY's own private distributed ledger and used it in an overnight triparty repo, with BNY acting as agent and cash lender and settlement taking minutes. Ledger Insights questioned whether a distributed ledger was needed, since BNY had launched intraday repo in early 2025 without one ([Ledger Insights, Oct 2026](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/bny-tokenizes-a-treasury-for-repo-the-question-is-why-it-needs-to/)). No published source describes a multi-bank collateral network on Besu where each participant runs a validator, which is the topology of the CDM prototype.

## Production systems keep private data off shared ledgers, and Paladin is still pre-V1

The Besu maintainers announced on 24 September 2024 that Tessera-based privacy would be removed because it was "not performant enough for their intended use cases" and had not kept up with Ethereum development, with a planned schedule of deprecation warnings, a freeze and removal over about seven months; the post names no release number and points to app-layer privacy and a planned LFDT Labs project ([LFDT blog](https://www.lfdecentralizedtrust.org/blog/sunsetting-tessera-and-simplifying-hyperledger-besu)). The Besu documentation now marks private transactions, privacy groups, flexible privacy groups and multi-tenancy as deprecated ([Besu docs](https://besu.hyperledger.org/private-networks/concepts/privacy/private-transactions)). No source found names a bank that used Tessera in production.

The production and near-production systems above handle privacy by network design. Citi runs an internal network; UBS and Deutsche Bank each ran their own network; the Swift ledger keeps balances on bank systems and holds obligation records; the DTCC AppChain keeps assets on their home ledgers **[LFDT report only]**; Agorá keeps reserves on per-jurisdiction ledgers. The Drex pilot is the one large test of privacy add-ons on a single shared Besu ledger with many participants, and it failed the central bank's requirements.

**Paladin** was announced as an LFDT Lab on 5 February 2025 with code from Kaleido. It records output state commitments on the base ledger after pre-executing transactions and collecting endorsement signatures, and it embeds Besu's EVM library without running a full Besu client ([LFDT blog: Announcing Paladin](https://www.lfdecentralizedtrust.org/blog/announcing-paladin-an-lf-decentralized-trust-lab-for-programmable-privacy-on-evm?hsLang=en)). The 2026 LFDT TAC annual review records that Paladin moved to Incubating in Q4 2025, shipped releases 0.14 to 0.16, had **not shipped V1** (beta planned for Q1-Q2 2026, full V1 "later in 2026" with a new distributed sequencing engine), had **all maintainers from Kaleido**, and listed no named adopters ([LFDT TAC review](https://lf-decentralized-trust.github.io/governance/project-updates/2026/2026-annual-paladin/)). A Ledger Insights claim that "multiple central and commercial banks" adopted it names none ([Ledger Insights, Nov 2025](https://ledgerinsights.com/paladin-privacy-framework-graduates-as-bank-of-korea-joins-lf-decentralized-trust/)). The documented uses are Agorá and unnamed testnet transactions in MAS Project Guardian.

The SUERF assessment of Agorá is the only published independent evaluation of Paladin. It found "strong confidentiality against non-involved participants", selective disclosure, low computational cost because there are no per-transaction zero-knowledge proofs, and that inconsistent views among group members become detectable. It also found that "only salted commitments are written on-chain" while validators and node operators can still observe the timing and number of commitment transactions, workflow stages, endorsement requests, group participation, exceptions and delays, which can reveal relationships or liquidity stress; that the Noto issuer or notary "becomes a concentration point for information and trust as well as availability"; that off-chain private state availability, recovery and key management are unresolved; that privacy overhead was not benchmarked; and that the July 2026 test "was not designed to test privacy properties" ([SUERF Note 420](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/privacy-in-wholesale-cross-border-payments-assessing-project-agora/)).

The Paladin documentation adds mechanism details. A Pente privacy group is a separate world state hosted inside one smart contract; its definition is distributed off-chain to members; the on-chain record holds salted hashes of the signed input and of input, output and read states; and the `transition` function takes EIP-712 signatures from all parties in the group. The group salt and member list must match what was stored in the group's genesis state, and no mechanism to add or remove members is described. Private messages between members carry "no ordering or non-repudiation guarantees" ([Paladin docs: Pente](https://lf-decentralized-trust-labs.github.io/paladin/head/architecture/pente)). A Noto token has one notary that validates and submits all transactions from its own account; Basic mode has fixed options for mint, burn and lock, where "only the creator of a lock may unlock it"; Hooks mode delegates policy to a private Pente contract, and a hook that neither emits the expected call nor reverts produces "undefined behavior" ([Paladin docs: Noto](https://lf-decentralized-trust-labs.github.io/paladin/head/architecture/noto)). Zeto uses zero-knowledge proofs, lets any party submit a valid transaction without a notary, and offers optional in-circuit encryption and an optional KYC registry ([Paladin docs: advanced crypto](https://lf-decentralized-trust-labs.github.io/paladin/head/concepts/advanced_crypto)). Regulators remain cautious about zero-knowledge proofs because the cryptography is new and audit skills are scarce **[LFDT report only]**, and the Brazilian central bank expressed similar caution ([LFDT CBDC ebook](https://www.lfdecentralizedtrust.org/hubfs/LFDT_CBDC%20ebook_V3-1.pdf?hsLang=en)).

## Applying the evidence to a per-deal Pente design for collateral

The visibility rule has three parts, and the evidence bears on each. The statements in this section are inferences from the sources above unless a citation is given.

**Deal participants see all deal data.** A Pente group per deal provides this directly, because members hold the private state and sign each transition. Since the group definition is fixed at creation, any event that changes the parties to a deal (novation, adding a triparty agent or custodian, a change of calculation agent) would require a new group and a migration of state from the old one. Because every member must sign every transition, any member that is offline blocks progress on that deal, which matters for time-critical steps such as margin calls and substitutions. Pente's lack of ordering and non-repudiation guarantees for private messages means that the CDM event sequence for a deal should be ordered by committed transitions, with off-chain messages treated only as notifications.

**An approved regulator may see some deal data.** Two configurations follow from the documented mechanism. If the regulator is a group member, it sees all deal data, which exceeds "some", and it becomes a required signer of every deal transition. If the regulator is outside the group, participants can disclose selected private states together with their salts, and the regulator can verify them against the salted hashes on the base ledger; SUERF lists selective disclosure as a property of the Agorá design ([SUERF Note 420](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/privacy-in-wholesale-cross-border-payments-assessing-project-agora/)). The Paladin pages reviewed do not describe a standard regulator-view feature, so the second configuration would be application code. Drex shows the cost of leaving this unresolved: the central bank rejected every privacy design in which the authority could not monitor or intervene ([BCB Drex phase 1 report](https://www.bcb.gov.br/content/estabilidadefinanceira/real_digital_docs/piloto/Relatorio_Drex_piloto_fase_1.pdf)). The prototype therefore needs a written statement of which CDM fields the regulator receives, when, through which channel, and whether the regulator needs any power to freeze or reverse.

**Everyone else sees nothing about the deal.** Under the current topology, every participant runs a QBFT validator, so every non-deal participant receives every base-ledger transaction. With Paladin, those participants would not see deal content, but they would see the deployment of each per-deal privacy group contract, the number and timing of transitions in each group, endorsement patterns, exceptions and delays, which SUERF identifies as able to reveal relationships and stress ([SUERF Note 420](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/privacy-in-wholesale-cross-border-payments-assessing-project-agora/)). Whether outsiders can link base-ledger submissions to specific institutions depends on submitter-key handling, which the fetched Paladin pages do not describe. The rule as written ("nothing") is stricter than what Paladin on a shared validator set provides; the options are to restate it as "nothing beyond commitment metadata", to add the mitigations SUERF lists (batching, randomized timing, padding, cover traffic), or to change which institutions run validators.

**Token choice.** A Noto notary sees all state of its token across all deals ([Paladin docs: Noto](https://lf-decentralized-trust-labs.github.io/paladin/head/architecture/noto)). If one Noto token class for cash or a security is shared across many deals, its notary sees every deal's collateral movements in that token, which breaks the third part of the rule unless the notary is a deal participant or the approved regulator. Noto's lock option maps to collateral encumbrance, and the DTCC AppChain's encumbrance model **[LFDT report only]** is the closest published precedent, but the Basic-mode rule that only the lock's creator can unlock it does not fit a default scenario in which the collateral receiver must take the asset; that case requires Hooks mode, where the documentation warns of undefined behavior on hook errors. Zeto removes the notary and its visibility. Zeto is a zero-knowledge token design in the same family as Anonymous Zether, which Brazil rejected because the authority could not track balances; the sources do not establish whether Zeto's optional in-circuit encryption can give a regulator that view. Agorá treated Zeto only as a benchmark ([SUERF Note 420](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/privacy-in-wholesale-cross-border-payments-assessing-project-agora/)).

**Operations, finality and performance.** Off-chain private state is the system of record for deal content under Paladin, and SUERF names its availability, backup and key recovery as the main condition for viability; each participant's Paladin node therefore needs backup and recovery tests before any regulator disclosure relies on it. The Agorá report distinguishes the "technical irreversibility of workflow state" that QBFT provides from settlement finality of the underlying funds ([BIS Agorá report](https://www.bis.org/publications/project-agora-shared-programmable-platform-wholesale-cross-border-payments.pdf)); Fnality's UK designation **[LFDT report only]** shows the legal route a production collateral system would need. The Besu maintainers' benchmark of **520 to 1,200 TPS on four QBFT validators** with 2-second blocks in one AWS availability zone ([Besu wiki](https://lf-hyperledger.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/BESU/pages/22156796/QBFT+Performance)) is above the message rate a collateral prototype is likely to need, but no source has measured Paladin overhead, and Drex measured a fall from about 150 to under 10 TPS with its privacy add-ons ([BIS-hosted speech](https://www.bis.org/review/r250703g.htm)). Paladin's pre-V1 status and the planned new sequencing engine mean the prototype should pin a Paladin version and expect API changes at V1. Tessera should not be used, since Besu has deprecated its privacy features.

## Conclusion

The evidence shows that banks put Besu into production when one institution, or a small regulated group, operates the ledger and when sensitive balances stay on that institution's own systems. A multi-party network where every participant validates and deal data is kept private on the shared chain has been tested only in Drex, which was retired, and in the Agorá prototype, which did not test privacy in its live run. A CDM collateral prototype that uses Pente groups per deal would therefore produce new evidence in an area with almost no published results, and it is most useful to the collateral subject-matter experts and partners if it measures what SUERF says was not measured: metadata exposure to non-deal validators, Paladin overhead, private-state recovery, and the regulator disclosure path.

The visibility rule is the design decision with the largest consequences. Brazil's experience shows that regulator access has to be specified before the privacy technology is chosen, and Agorá's experience shows that a notary-based token gives one party full visibility of that token. Writing the rule down at field level, deciding whether the regulator is a group member or a recipient of selective disclosure, and accepting or mitigating base-ledger metadata exposure should come before the Noto-or-Zeto choice.
